## What to remove or de‑emphasize

Assuming a typical “20‑year‑old” club site, these elements often age badly and tend to clutter more than help:

- **Long, unstructured history sections**  
  - Keep a short “Our story” paragraph (3–5 sentences) and maybe one photo.  
  - Move detailed chronicles, old tournament reports, and long lists of past members to an optional “Archive” page or PDF.

- **Outdated event listings**  
  - Any fixed HTML lists of past tournaments, old news from years ago, or “upcoming” events that are clearly stale.  
  - Replace with a dynamic “News & Events” section you can actually maintain.

- **Overly technical rule expositions on the front page**  
  - Full rulebooks, long essays on joseki, or deep strategy articles should not dominate the homepage.  
  - Keep a simple “What is Go?” section with a short explanation and a link to a dedicated “Learn Go” page.

- **Dense text walls without visual breaks**  
  - Big blocks of text with no subheadings, bullets, or images.  
  - Replace with short paragraphs, clear headings, and occasional photos of your club in action.

- **Outdated design artifacts**  
  - Hit counters, “Best viewed in Netscape/IE”, animated GIFs, blinking text, frame-based layouts, etc.  
  - These signal “old site” immediately and reduce trust, especially for newcomers and parents.

- **Redundant contact info scattered everywhere**  
  - If your address, email, and meeting times appear on every page in different formats, consolidate them.  
  - Use a single, clear “Contact & Location” section and a footer with essential info.

## What to add or restructure

Think of your website’s main jobs:

1. Tell people **what the club is** in 10 seconds.  
2. Make it trivial to **show up and play**.  
3. Help beginners **learn the basics** and feel welcome.  
4. Keep current members informed about **events and news**.  
5. Present the club as **active, friendly, and credible**.

With that in mind, consider these content elements:

### 1. A strong, simple homepage

Homepage should answer immediately:

- **Who you are**: “Lubelski Klub Go – spotkania, nauka i turnieje w Lublinie” (or similar).  
- **When & where**: Day, time, and location clearly visible in the first screen (e.g., “Środy 17:00–20:00, Młodzieżowy Dom Kultury nr 2, Lublin”).  
- **Who can join**: “Zapraszamy dzieci, młodzież i dorosłych – od zupełnych początkujących po zaawansowanych graczy.”  
- **Call to action**: “Przyjdź na pierwsze spotkanie”, “Napisz do nas”, or “Zobacz, jak zacząć”.

Include:

- 1–2 good photos of people playing at your club (real members, real boards).  
- A short “What is Go?” box (2–4 sentences) with a link to a fuller explanation.  
- A small “Upcoming events” highlight (next tournament, workshop, or open day).

### 2. Clear “How to start” path for beginners

Create a dedicated **“Zacznij grać w Go”** (or “Nauka Go”) section with:

- Very short intro: what Go is, why it’s interesting, who plays.  
- Basic rules in simple language (no heavy jargon), ideally with diagrams.  
- A suggested learning path:
  - First meeting: learn the rules and play a 9×9 game.  
  - Next steps: 13×13, basic life & death, simple endgame.  
- Links to good external resources (OGS, BadukPop, Go tutorials, etc.).  
- FAQ for beginners:
  - “Czy muszę znać zasady przed pierwszym spotkaniem?”  
  - “Czy są jakieś opłaty?”  
  - “Czy są grupy wiekowe?”  
  - “Czy macie kamienie i plansze na miejscu?”

This is often the most important page for growing the club.

### 3. Practical “Meetings & Location” page

Make it extremely easy to find:

- Regular meeting schedule (day(s), hours).  
- Exact location:
  - Name of the venue (e.g., MDK nr 2).  
  - Address.  
  - A map embed (Google Maps or OpenStreetMap).  
  - Short directions (e.g., “2nd floor, room X; look for Go boards near the entrance”).  
- Contact person(s):
  - Name, role (e.g., “organizator spotkań”), email, maybe phone if appropriate.  
- Notes for special cases:
  - Holiday schedule changes.  
  - What to do if a meeting is cancelled (where you’ll post updates—email, Facebook, etc.).

### 4. News & Events section

Instead of a static “Tournaments” page, use a **News & Events** section:

- Upcoming:
  - Regular club nights.  
  - Internal tournaments.  
  - Workshops (e.g., “Podstawy życia i śmierci dla początkujących”).  
  - Participation in external events (e.g., Mistrzostwa Polski, local festivals).  
- Past:
  - Short reports with a few photos and key results (winners, number of participants).  
  - No need for exhaustive move-by-move coverage unless you want a separate blog.

Keep each item short and scannable: date, title, 2–4 sentences, maybe a photo.

### 5. Club identity & community

People join clubs, not just games. Show the human side:

- Short “O klubie” page:
  - Mission in 2–3 sentences (e.g., promoting Go in Lublin, supporting players of all levels).  
  - Very brief history (founding year, key milestones).  
  - Mention partnerships (e.g., MDK nr 2, schools, cultural centers).  
- Photos:
  - Group photos from meetings/tournaments.  
  - Candid shots of people playing, teaching kids, etc.  
- Optional: short member stories or quotes:
  - “Why I play Go” from a few members (students, adults, seniors).  
  - Especially valuable if you want to attract families or schools.

### 6. Resources & learning materials

You don’t need to create everything yourself, but a curated **Resources** page is valuable:

- Recommended sites for learning Go (in Polish and English).  
- Apps and servers (OGS, KGS, BadukPop, etc.).  
- Books (with short notes: “good for absolute beginners”, “for intermediate players”).  
- Any materials you’ve created:
  - PDFs from workshops.  
  - Local “house rules” for internal tournaments.  
  - Problem sets (tsumego) you use in training.

This also helps with SEO and positions your club as a serious educational resource.

### 7. For schools, teachers, and parents

Since you’re an educator and you already cooperate with MDK and possibly schools, consider a small section aimed at them:

- “Go w szkołach” or “Dla nauczycieli i rodziców”:
  - Short explanation of educational benefits (logic, concentration, strategic thinking).  
  - Examples of activities you run (workshops, school visits, tournaments for kids).  
  - How a school can collaborate:
    - One-off workshops.  
    - Regular after-school Go club.  
    - Participation in youth tournaments.  
  - Contact info specifically for this type of cooperation.

Even a single well-written page here can open doors.

### 8. Clear contact & social links

Make it trivial to reach you:

- Dedicated **Contact** page with:
  - General email.  
  - Name(s) of contact person(s).  
  - Links to social media (Facebook, Instagram, Discord, OGS club page, etc.), if you use them.  
  - Simple contact form (optional, but nice).  
- Footer on every page with:
  - Meeting day/time.  
  - Venue name and city.  
  - Email.  
  - Social icons.

You can keep the top menu to 5–6 items and put some of these as subpages.

## Feature ideas that could make your site stand out

Beyond standard club info, here are some content/features that could give your site a unique character:

### 1. “First time at our club?” mini-guide

A short, friendly page or box:

- What to expect at your first meeting.  
- What time to arrive.  
- Whether you need to know the rules.  
- Whether there are fees.  
- Who will greet you.  
- A photo of the room so people recognize it.

This reduces anxiety for newcomers, especially teens and adults who’ve never been to a board-game club.

### 2. Regular “Problem of the Month”

A small recurring feature:

- One tsumego (life & death problem) per month, with difficulty level.  
- Diagram + question (“Czarne zaczynają – czy mogą żyć?”).  
- Solution revealed after a few weeks or on a separate page.  

This:
- Gives people a reason to revisit the site.  
- Creates a light “club tradition”.  
- Can be promoted on social media.

### 3. Member spotlights / interviews

Occasional short posts:

- “Gracz miesiąca” or “Portret członka klubu”.  
- 5–8 questions: how they found Go, favorite aspect, advice for beginners, etc.  
- Photo if they’re comfortable.

This builds community and makes the site feel alive.

### 4. Simple online presence for your club

Even if you don’t build a full platform, you can:

- Link to an OGS club page or Baduk.club profile.  
- Show your club’s OGS/Baduk.club link prominently (“Graj z nami online”).  
- Optionally embed a small widget or list of active members (if available).

This bridges offline and online play, which is important nowadays.

### 5. Gallery with context

Instead of a random photo dump:

- Small themed galleries:
  - “Turniej X – 2024”  
  - “Warsztaty dla dzieci”  
  - “Nasze cotygodniowe spotkania”  
- Each gallery with a short caption explaining what’s happening.

Photos of real people and real moments are powerful for attracting new members.

### 6. Calendar view of events

Even a simple calendar (could be Google Calendar embedded) showing:

- Regular weekly meetings.  
- Upcoming tournaments and workshops.  
- Any breaks (holidays, summer pause).

This is more user-friendly than long textual lists.

### 7. “Go in Lublin” hub

Position your site as the main Go resource in the region:

- Mention other local Go-related activities (if any).  
- List regional tournaments you attend.  
- Provide info for visitors (“If you’re in Lublin and want to play Go, join us on Wednesdays…”).

Over time, you could even host a small annual event and make the site its natural home.

## Content tone and style suggestions

To make the site feel modern and welcoming:

- Use **plain, friendly Polish**, avoiding overly formal or academic tone.  
- Address the reader directly (“Ty” form) where appropriate, especially on beginner pages.  
- Keep sections short and scannable:
  - Clear headings.  
  - Bullet points for key info.  
  - Short paragraphs (3–5 sentences max).  
- Emphasize inclusivity:
  - “Nie musisz znać zasad.”  
  - “Zapraszamy osoby w każdym wieku i na każdym poziomie.”
