Absolutely. I looked at the current site first, including the homepage and the separate rules page. ([Lubelski Klub GO][1])

My main reaction is that I **wouldn't try to modernize the existing site page-by-page**. I would treat it as a source of information and rebuild the content architecture around what a visitor actually wants to know.

Right now the site essentially answers:

> *"Where and when do you meet, and what is Go?"*

A new site could answer a much more useful set of questions:

> **"What is this club? Would I enjoy it? Can I just show up? What happens when I come? How do I learn? How do I get in touch? What's happening soon?"**

Here's how I'd approach it.

---

# 1. What I would keep

There is actually some good information on the current page.

  * Wednesday
  * 17:00–20:00

  * Młodzieżowy Dom Kultury nr 2

Those are the core facts someone needs.

The current site makes the meeting information particularly prominent, which is good. ([Lubelski Klub GO][1])

I'd just present all of it in a much more contemporary and inviting way.

# 2. What I would remove or drastically reduce

## ❌ The giant "rules of Go" article

This is the biggest thing I'd reconsider.

The existing rules page is essentially a long technical explanation of liberties, atari, eyes, ko, dead stones, suicide, territory, etc. ([Lubelski Klub GO][2])

That's perfectly legitimate information, but **it's not the best way to introduce someone to the club**.

Imagine someone who has never played Go arriving from Google.

They probably don't want to read a 30-paragraph explanation before deciding whether to come.

I'd replace it with something like:

### "New to Go?"

> Go is a two-player strategy game played with black and white stones.
> The rules are surprisingly simple, but the game has enormous strategic depth.
>
> **Never played? No problem. We'll teach you.**
>
> You can learn the basics in a few minutes and play your first game at the club.

Then perhaps:

**Learn the rules →**

leading to a separate, properly designed "Learn Go" section.

The detailed material can still exist. It just shouldn't be the primary introduction.

# 3. I would make the homepage much more human

The current site starts with the club name, an illustration and then immediately goes into logistics. ([Lubelski Klub GO][1])

I'd instead make the first screen communicate the *experience*.

Something along the lines of:

# Lubelski Klub Go

### Play. Learn. Meet people.

**Every Wednesday · 17:00–20:00**
Młodzieżowy Dom Kultury nr 2 · Lublin

**Everyone is welcome — including complete beginners.**

[ **Come play with us** ]
[ **I'm new to Go** ]

Then a nice photograph/illustration of people actually playing Go.

That last point is important.

I'd strongly favor **real photographs from the club** over generic Go imagery.

The site should make someone think:

> *"Oh, these are actual people. I could go there."*

rather than:

> *"This is a website explaining an abstract board game."*

# 4. Add a very clear "Your first visit" section

This is something I think could make a surprisingly big difference.

A newcomer often has questions they won't necessarily ask:

* Do I need to register?
* Do I need to bring a Go board?
* Do I need to know the rules?
* Do I need to bring a friend?
* Is it really free?
* What if I'm a child?
* What if I'm an adult beginner?
* Will someone actually teach me?
* Can I just walk in?
* What level are the other players?

I'd have a section called:

## First time at the club?

**You don't need to know how to play.**

1. Come to the club on Wednesday.
2. Find us in room 14.
3. Tell us you're new.
4. We'll explain the basics and find someone to play with.

**No registration. No membership fee. No previous experience required.**

Obviously, we'd verify which of those statements actually apply before publishing them.

This kind of information is much more valuable to a prospective newcomer than a long explanation of ko.

The current website barely communicates that there is a **community** behind it.

I'd change that.

Something like:

## A club for people who like thinking — and playing together

Then a few photographs and short descriptions.

Perhaps:

* Club organizers
* Regular players
* Young players

* Tournament players
* Beginners

Not necessarily formal biographies.

Even something as simple as:

> **Our club brings together beginners and experienced players, children and adults, casual players and tournament competitors.**

would make the organization feel alive.

# 6. Give the club an actual "personality"

This is probably the biggest content opportunity.

The current site tells me **what the club does**, but not really **why I should want to belong to it**.

I'd want the new site to answer:

### What kind of place is this?

Is it:

* serious and competitive?
* relaxed and social?
* family-oriented?
* focused on teaching?
* focused on tournaments?
* a mixture of all of these?

You don't necessarily need to choose one.

In fact, I suspect the interesting story could be:

> **You can come here simply to play a game, or you can take Go as seriously as you want.**

That gives the club room for different kinds of people.

# 20. What I would *not* put on the homepage

I'd avoid turning the homepage into a giant information dump.

I wouldn't put:

* detailed Go rules
* long history
* extensive tournament regulations
* huge lists of links
* technical terminology
* enormous photo galleries
* walls of text
* every Facebook/Discord announcement
* outdated announcements

The homepage should answer **four things almost immediately**:

> **What is this?**
> **Who is it for?**
> **When/where is it?**
> **Can I just come?**

Everything else can branch off from there.

# 22. My biggest content change

If I had to summarize my approach in one sentence:

### I'd change the site from an *information page about a Go club* into an *invitation to join a Go community*.

The existing site is very much:

> **"Here are the facts."**

The new one should be:

> **"This is who we are, this is what happens here, and we'd love you to come and play."**

And importantly, **without becoming cheesy or corporate**. A Go club should still feel like a Go club.

## One more thing I'd explore

There is an interesting opportunity to make the website itself feel somewhat like **Go**, rather than simply putting a Go logo on a conventional modern website.

For example, the visual/content language could revolve around:

* black and white stones
* intersections
* territory
* subtle grid structures
* strategic "moves"
* board positions as visual elements
* restrained Japanese-inspired aesthetics without becoming stereotypically "Asian"
* photography of actual club members
* lots of whitespace

That could give the site a distinctive identity rather than making it look like another generic modern club website.
